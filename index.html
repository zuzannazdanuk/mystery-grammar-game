Case Study — Gamified Learning

Inspector Grammar:
The Golden Whistle

A mystery narrative game teaching Active and Passive Voice to 10–13 year olds — where every grammar exercise is a clue and every correct answer moves the investigation forward.

🔍 Mystery Narrative 🎮 HTML5 Game 👦 Ages 10–13 📖 Active vs Passive Voice ⚙️ Vanilla JS
01 — Overview

Grammar as a crime to solve

Inspector Grammar: The Golden Whistle Mystery is a five-screen browser-based game that teaches Active and Passive Voice through a detective narrative. Instead of presenting grammar rules and asking students to apply them in isolation, the game embeds every exercise inside a story — the theft of a golden referee's whistle from a sports hall.

Each screen advances the investigation. Sorting sentences correctly reveals the next witness. Fixing grammar errors in testimony exposes a suspect. Converting active notes to passive voice produces the lab report that cracks the case. The grammar is the gameplay.

"What if understanding passive voice was the only thing standing between you and solving a crime? That's the motivational architecture this game is built on."
02 — The Problem

Why grammar exercises stop working

Active and Passive Voice is one of the most commonly taught — and most commonly misunderstood — grammar topics for learners aged 10–13. The concept itself isn't complex: who is doing the action, and who is receiving it. But standard instruction tends to present it as a transformation exercise — here is an active sentence, now rewrite it in passive — stripped of any context for why you'd ever want to do that.

The result is students who can complete a worksheet in class and make the same error in their next written piece. The knowledge doesn't transfer because it was never connected to anything meaningful.

Motivation is not a reward at the end of the exercise. It's the reason to start it in the first place.

A second problem specific to this age group: attention. 10–13 year olds have high tolerance for complexity when it's embedded in something they find engaging, and very low tolerance for abstract rules with no apparent purpose. The format needed to hold their attention without reducing the cognitive demand.

03 — The Design Solution

Five screens, one case

The game is structured as five sequential screens, each with a distinct learning function. The narrative provides continuity — every screen feels like the next chapter of the same story, not a new exercise.

1
The Crime Scene — Sort & Identify
Four witness statements must be sorted into Active or Passive Voice columns by dragging or clicking. Inspector Grammar introduces the case and the grammar distinction simultaneously — the student learns the rule through the act of sorting, not before it.
Drag & Drop · Recognition
2
Interrogation — Spot & Fix
Mr. Jones the caretaker has made two errors in his written testimony — one logical (subject and object reversed), one grammatical (passive used incorrectly). Students must identify and rewrite both in correct Active Voice. Text input with case-insensitive validation and collapsible hints.
Text Input · Production · Error Correction
3
The Choice — Branching Narrative
Two evidence options are presented: Security Cameras (Active Voice) and Fingerprints Lab (Passive Voice). The camera footage is locked — a deliberate design choice that channels the learner toward the passive voice exercise while making it feel like a narrative decision, not a forced task.
Branching · Narrative Agency
4
Lab Analysis — Active to Passive
Three forensic notes written in Active Voice must be rewritten in Passive Voice for the official court report. The context — a lab report, not a grammar exercise — gives the transformation real-world purpose. Each note has an expandable hint explaining the formula.
Text Input · Transformation · Production
5
Conclusion — Victory & Cliffhanger
The fingerprints are confirmed as Mr. Jones's. A summary of evidence confirms what was learned. A new clue — a piece of sports fabric — sets up Part 2 and rewards completion with narrative curiosity rather than just a score.
Celebration · Narrative Hook · Replay Motivation
04 — Pedagogical Thinking

The learning design behind the story

The five screens follow a deliberate progression from recognition to production — a sequence grounded in cognitive load theory and second language acquisition research.

Recognise (Screen 1) → Correct (Screen 2) → Choose (Screen 3) → Transform (Screen 4) → Consolidate (Screen 5)

Recognition comes first because it requires the lowest cognitive load — students identify a pattern before they need to produce one. Error correction in Screen 2 raises the stakes: students must not only identify the wrong form but understand why it's wrong and construct the correct alternative. Transformation in Screen 4 is the most demanding task and arrives only after two prior exposures to the concept.

🔒

Forgiving validation

All text inputs ignore capitalisation and punctuation. A correct answer is correct regardless of whether the student typed a full stop or remembered to capitalise "I".

💡

Collapsible hints

Every exercise has an optional hint that can be revealed without penalty. This supports learners who are stuck without removing the productive struggle for those who aren't.

⚡

Instant feedback

Every answer triggers immediate visual feedback — green for correct, red with an explanation for incorrect. No waiting, no ambiguity about what went wrong.

🎭

Narrative motivation

Each correct answer advances the story. The student isn't completing a grammar exercise — they're solving a crime. The motivation is intrinsic to the task, not attached to it.

📱

Touch-first design

Drag and drop works on both desktop and mobile. A click-based fallback ensures the sorting mechanic functions on touchscreens without modification.

📊

Progress visibility

A gold progress bar at the top of every screen shows how far through the investigation the student is — making the endpoint visible and reducing abandonment.

The locked camera footage in Screen 3 is not a bug — it's a design decision. The student is given the illusion of choice while being directed toward the passive voice exercise. This preserves narrative agency (the student feels like they're making a decision) while ensuring the learning objective is met. It also mirrors real detective work, where not every lead is viable.

05 — Development Approach

One file, all the complexity

The entire game — all five screens, all CSS, all JavaScript — lives in a single HTML file. This was a deliberate constraint: the game needed to be deployable anywhere, shareable as a single link, and openable without any server, framework, or installation.

My role was the instructional and narrative architecture. I defined the learning objectives, designed the screen sequence, wrote the scenario and all sentence content, specified the validation logic (case-insensitive, punctuation-agnostic), and directed the UX behaviour for every interaction. The implementation was handled through AI-assisted code generation — the same philosophy that underpins my broader approach to instructional design: use technology to execute, keep human judgment for the design.

06 — Key Takeaways

What this project demonstrates

🎮

Gamification with genuine pedagogical purpose

Every game mechanic — sorting, text input, branching, transformation — maps directly to a specific learning objective. Nothing was added for entertainment value alone.

📖

Narrative as a motivational architecture

The detective story isn't decoration. It provides the intrinsic motivation to complete each exercise, the context that makes the grammar meaningful, and the reward structure that makes completion satisfying.

🧠

Cognitive load sequencing

Recognition → error correction → transformation follows a deliberate progression from lower to higher cognitive demand, ensuring each screen prepares the learner for the next challenge.

🤗

Learner-centred UX for the 10–13 age group

Forgiving validation, collapsible hints, instant feedback, and touch-first interaction all reflect specific design decisions based on the cognitive and emotional needs of the target age group.

🤖

Strategic AI-assisted development

The instructional architecture, scenario writing, and UX logic were designed by me. AI handled the technical implementation — demonstrating efficient, modern workflow without sacrificing design quality.

🌍

Subject-matter expertise embedded in design

The sentence choices, error types, and passive/active examples were selected for their pedagogical value — not as filler content. Four years of English teaching informed every grammar decision in the game.
